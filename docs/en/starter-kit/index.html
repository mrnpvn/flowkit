The 30-minute guide

Five steps. If you are stuck on step one, stop: almost every failed automation project started by choosing the wrong first task.

1. Pick a task, not a department

Not “automate marketing”. Pick something you do three or more times a week that can be described in one sentence: “move the details from this email into the sheet”, “answer the first message from a new client”, “summarise today's three invoices”. If it does not fit in one sentence, it is a project, not a task.

2. Time it once, properly

Do it with a clock and write the minutes down. Most people think they spend 2 minutes and spend 9. Without that number you cannot tell whether automating is worth it: a 2-minute task every day is 8 hours a year; a 15-minute one is 60.

3. Write the rule before you open the tool

On paper or in a note: when it fires, what goes in, what comes out, and what happens when something fails. If you cannot write the failure path, you are not automating: you are hiding an error. That rule is what later becomes nodes, filters and branches.

4. Build the ugly version in a day

The first version has to work with one real case, not ten. A form, a spreadsheet and one notification is already an automation. The pretty version comes later — and often never comes, because nobody needs it.

5. Measure the saving and add a watchdog

Record how many times it ran and how many minutes it saved. Then add an alert for failures: a silent automation that breaks is worse than the manual task, because you still believe it is done.

The one rule that prevents 80% of disasters

If the automation moves money, changes client data, or writes into a system other people depend on, do not let it run unattended for the first month. Make it notify you before acting, and review the first ten cases by hand. Automating a mistake only makes it faster.

The 10 prompts

Copy the text, replace what is in brackets, paste it into whichever assistant you use. They are written to force a concrete answer: if the model replies with theory, the bracketed context was missing.

To choose well

1 · Map of repetitive tasks

“Act as [your role] in [type of business]. I will describe a typical week; turn it into a table with: task, times per week, minutes per time, whether it depends on another person, and whether a mistake would have serious consequences. Then sort it by minutes per month and tell me which one I should automate first and why. My week: [describe 8-10 real tasks].”

2 · The real cost of a task

“Calculate the annual cost of this task: it happens [N] times a week, takes [M] minutes, and the person doing it costs [hourly cost] per hour. Give me the total in hours and in money, and compare it with the cost of automating it if setup takes [X] hours and maintenance [Y] hours a year. Tell me in which month the investment pays back and list the assumptions you used.”

For content

3 · One idea, five formats

“I have this idea: [idea in two sentences]. Turn it into five pieces without repeating the same text: a 120-word email, a short social post, three slide bullets, an FAQ section, and a 45-second video script. Keep the same argument and change the format. Do not invent data or numbers.”

4 · Rewrite for one specific client

“Rewrite this text for [client type: profile, technical level, what they worry about]. Rules: short sentences, no buzzwords, no promises that are not in the original, same level of detail. At the end, tell me what you changed and why. Original text: [paste the text].”

To sell

5 · Answer “it is too expensive” without discounting

“A client replied: [paste their message]. My price is [price] and I do not want to lower it. Write three different replies: one that reframes the value, one that reduces the scope so it fits their budget, and one that accepts losing the project without damaging the relationship. Tone: direct, not servile. 90 words maximum each.”

6 · Follow-up on a quote you sent

“I sent a quote for [amount] to [client type] [days] ago. Write three follow-up messages spaced [7, 14 and 25] days apart. Each one must add something new (a resolved doubt, a similar case, a real deadline) and must not sound like pressure. Subject and body, under 100 words.”

For operations

7 · Turn a messy email into data

“Extract the client details from this email as a table with these columns: name, company, email, phone, what they ask for, deadline, amount if it appears. If a field is missing, write EMPTY instead of inventing it. At the end, list what is missing before I can reply. Email: [paste the email].”

8 · A meeting summary that is actually useful

“Turn these notes into three blocks: decisions made, tasks with an owner and a date, and open questions. Do not summarise what was discussed if it produced no decision. If a task has no owner or date, mark it as UNASSIGNED. Notes: [paste the notes].”

So you do not break what already works

9 · Weekly audit of your automations

“These are my active automations: [list what each does and when]. Review which ones can fail silently, which depend on one person, which write data nobody checks, and which duplicate another one. Return a table with high/medium/low risk and the first check I should do on each.”

10 · Post-mortem without looking for someone to blame

“Something went wrong: [what happened, when, and the consequences]. Apply the five whys until you reach a cause that can be fixed by a process change, not a people change. Finish with three concrete actions, who does them, and how we will know they are done.”

Checklist: what to automate first

Score each task from 1 to 3 on the five rows. Add them up. You do not need a spreadsheet: five tasks and five numbers already show which one goes first.

Criterion1 point2 points3 points
FrequencyLess than once a week2-4 times a weekDaily or more
Time per runUnder 2 minutes2-10 minutesOver 10 minutes
Always the same stepsEvery case is differentSmall variationsExactly the same steps
Cost of a mistakeFixed in a minuteCosts an apologyAffects money or a client

How to read the score

12-15 points: automate it this week; the return is obvious. 8-11: automate it after the first one, once something is already running. 5-7: leave it manual; building and maintaining it costs more than doing it. Watch the last row: a task that only one person can do scores high on dependency, but if a mistake is also expensive, it goes last. Document it first, automate it second.
